## When to use
Any document for a senior, time-poor reader who must decide, approve or be informed: decision memos, board and steering papers, status updates, escalations, executive summaries of long reports, emails to leadership.

## Core structures
- BLUF (bottom line up front): the first sentence gives the conclusion, the ask or the decision needed, with the deadline. The term comes from US Army correspondence rules (AR 25-50, edition of 10 October 2020), which put the main point at the start.
- Minto pyramid: one governing thought answers the reader's question; 2-5 key-line points sit directly under it; each is supported by the level below. Vertical logic: every level answers the "why?" or "how?" that the level above raises. Horizontal logic: a group is either a deductive chain (premise, premise, therefore) or an inductive set of like ideas; inductive sets are MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) and ordered on purpose (time, structure or importance).
- SCQA opening: Situation (context the reader already accepts), Complication (what changed or went wrong), Question (what this raises for the reader), Answer (the governing thought). Keep S and C to a sentence or two each; for executives put the Answer first.

## Method
1. Write the reader's question in one line and your answer in one sentence of 25 words or fewer. If you cannot, you are not ready to write.
2. Build the pyramid: governing thought, three key reasons or actions (rarely more than five), evidence under each. Test each group for MECE and each bullet for "so what?".
3. Draft a one-page memo with the template below; push detail into annexes.
4. Quantify impact in money, time, risk or customers, with units, period and source; prefer ranges to false precision.
5. Cut throat-clearing ("This memo aims to..."), stacked hedges and anything the decision does not depend on. Use message headings ("Option B halves cost but adds three months").

## Decision memo template
1. Decision required: what, from whom, by when.
2. Recommendation and the one-line reason.
3. Context: SCQ in three to five sentences.
4. Options table: two to four real options including the status quo; columns for description, benefit, cost, risk, time and reversibility, on the same criteria.
5. Why the recommendation wins and why the others lose; key assumptions and what would change the answer.
6. Risks and mitigations, dependencies, and what you need from the reader.
7. Next steps with owners and dates. Annex: data, method, sources.

## Checklist
- Could the reader act after the first paragraph alone?
- Exactly one ask, phrased as a yes/no or a choice.
- Options are distinct, feasible and compared on the same criteria; no straw men.
- Figures match across summary, body and annex.
- Bad news appears early and plainly, with what is being done about it.

## Pitfalls
- Chronological story ("First we... then we...") instead of the answer.
- The ask buried at the end, or several asks in one memo.
- Key-line points that overlap or are not the same kind of idea.
- A recommendation with no statement of the cost of being wrong.
