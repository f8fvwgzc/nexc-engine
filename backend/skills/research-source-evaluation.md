## When to use
Before a source's claim enters a finding, and whenever two sources conflict.

## SIFT, adapted to the workspace
1. Stop. Before using a claim, ask whether you know this source and its standing. A strong emotional pull or a claim that is too convenient is a cue to slow down.
2. Investigate the source. Judge it by what others say about it, not by its own self-description: lateral reading. Fact checkers who leave a page quickly to check its owner outperform careful readers who stay on it. Here: check other workspace documents, the source's own references and your background knowledge (marked unverified).
3. Find better coverage. Look for a more authoritative or more primary source on the same claim; if one exists, cite it instead.
4. Trace claims, quotes and figures to the original context. Check the number, unit, date, population and definition at the origin; secondary retellings often drop the caveats.

## Criteria
- Authority: named author or organisation, relevant expertise, accountability (an editor, regulator or auditor stands behind it).
- Currency: publication and data dates; superseded editions; whether the topic moves fast.
- Accuracy: methods described, figures reproducible, internally consistent, matches independent sources.
- Purpose and bias: inform, sell or persuade; funding; conflicts of interest; advocacy framing; selective time windows.
- Review: peer-reviewed, editorially controlled, preprint, or self-published; check for retractions or corrections noted in the workspace.
- Provenance of data: who collected it, how, when, sample size and frame, definitions, transformations, and whether raw data are available.
- Access and motivation (ICD 203): did the source have direct access, and what does it gain if believed?

## Tiers
| Tier | Typical sources |
|---|---|
| A | official text of a law or standard, audited filings, official statistics with methods, systematic reviews |
| B | peer-reviewed studies, established reference works, primary internal records |
| C | reputable press, analyst reports that state methods, trade bodies |
| D | vendor material, press releases, blogs, undated or anonymous pages |
| E | unverifiable, circular, or machine-generated text without sources |
Rate the claim separately from the source: a tier A source can be misquoted, and a tier D source can be right on its own product's specs.

## Decision rules
- A key figure needs a tier A or B source, or two independent tier C sources; otherwise state it with lower confidence and say why.
- Use tier D for what the publisher says about itself (its prices, specs, stated plans), labelled as such, never for market-wide facts.
- In a conflict, prefer the more primary, more recent source that states its method; record the one you set aside and the reason.

## Red flags
No date or author; round numbers with no method; tiny or self-selected samples; survivorship in case studies; two sources citing one origin; a figure that grows as it is retold.

## Pitfalls
- Judging by look, logos or domain name instead of lateral checks.
- Treating peer review as proof, or a vendor source as automatically wrong.
- Forgetting to re-check currency when an edition or dataset has been updated.
