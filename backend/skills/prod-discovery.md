## When to use
A team has an outcome to move or a solution someone wants built, and the problem, customer or value is not yet proven.

## Method
1. Set the outcome: a product outcome (a customer behaviour the team can influence), not an output and not a distant business number.
2. Interview weekly as a product trio (product, design, engineering). Revisit the opportunity map every three or four interviews.
3. Map opportunities (needs, pains, desires) under the moments of the customer's journey. Reframe any requested feature as the problem behind it.
4. Choose one target opportunity by sizing (how many customers, how often), customer importance and satisfaction with today's options, market factors and company fit. Do not score opportunities on effort at this stage.
5. Generate at least three solutions for that opportunity and compare them.
6. List the assumptions behind each solution and test the riskiest first.
7. Decide: build, refine, try another solution, pick another opportunity, or stop.

## Interviews
- Ask for a specific past story: "Tell me about the last time you..." Probe what happened, who was involved, what they used, what it cost them.
- Avoid hypotheticals ("would you use...?"), leading questions and pitching. Past behaviour beats stated intent.
- Recruit people who have the behaviour, including recent switchers and those who left.
- Capture an interview snapshot: key quote, context facts, opportunities heard, a small journey map.

## Jobs to be done
- A job is the progress a person wants in a circumstance; it has functional, social and emotional sides.
- Competition includes workarounds, spreadsheets and doing nothing.
- Forces on a switch: push of the current situation, pull of the new solution, anxiety about the new, habit of the present.
- Job story: "When <situation>, I want to <motivation>, so I can <outcome>."

## Assumption testing
- Risk types: value (will they choose it), usability (can they use it), feasibility (can we build it), business viability (does it work for sales, legal, finance, brand); also ask whether it should be built at all (ethical risk).
- Map assumptions by importance and evidence; test the important ones with weak evidence.
- Write the test before running it: "We believe <assumption>. We will see <metric> reach <threshold> among <n> <segment> by <date>."
- Cheapest test that answers the question: existing data, one-question survey, fake door, concierge, Wizard of Oz, clickable prototype with 5 users per round for usability, live-data prototype for value, technical spike for feasibility.

## Output
Discovery brief: outcome; target customer; opportunity map snapshot; evidence (number of interviews, segments, dates, quotes); assumption table (assumption, type, evidence, test, result); decision and next tests. Public-sector discoveries often take 4-8 weeks, build nothing, quantify the cost of the problem, and may end in a decision to stop.

## Pitfalls
Solutions disguised as opportunities; one big research batch then none; confirmation bias in synthesis; counting compliments as evidence; tests with no pass criteria; sales anecdotes treated as demand without context.
