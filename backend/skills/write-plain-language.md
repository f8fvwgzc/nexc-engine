## When to use
- Text a non-specialist must act on: notices, letters, policies, instructions, forms, UI text, emails, summaries of technical or legal material.
- Requests to "make this clearer", readability reviews, drafting from notes.

## Key standards
- ISO 24495-1:2023 (June 2023): four interdependent principles. Readers get what they need (relevant), can easily find it (findable), can understand it (understandable) and can use it (usable). Word choice is only one part: plan for the reader's purpose, structure for finding, and check use with real readers.
- ISO 24495-2:2025 (August 2025) applies the same principles to legal communication.
- US Plain Writing Act of 2010 (federal agencies). plainlanguage.gov was taken down after September 2025 (verify locally) and now redirects to Digital.gov's plain language guides; those guides are not the Federal Plain Language Guidelines, whose last version the Center for Plain Language reposts.
- ISO 24495-1 leaves accessibility to existing technical guidance (it points to WCAG and EN 301 549): apply WCAG 2.2 separately for digital text.

## Method
1. Reader and task: name the primary reader, what they already know, what they must do or decide afterwards, and where they read (phone, print, form).
2. Content: keep what the reader needs to act; move background and rare cases to an annex or link; delete the rest.
3. Structure: main message or required action in the first two sentences; order sections by the reader's questions or task sequence; descriptive headings ("How to appeal", not "Appeals"); one topic per section.
4. Sentences: one idea each; aim for an average of 15-20 words and split or list anything over about 25 (a rule of thumb; ISO 24495-1 sets no word limit). Active voice with the actor named ("We will review your claim"); passive only when the actor is unknown or irrelevant. Present tense by default; "you" for the reader, "we" for the organisation; "must" for obligations instead of "shall".
5. Words: everyday words (use, not utilise; start, not commence); turn hidden verbs back into verbs ("make a decision" -> "decide"; watch -tion, -ment, -ance); define unavoidable terms once at first use; one term per concept; spell out or drop acronyms.
6. Lists and tables: numbered lists for steps in order; bullets for unordered items (about 3-7, parallel grammar, with a lead-in); tables to compare options across attributes or to show "if X, then Y" rules.
7. Test usability: can a representative reader find the answer and do the task? Use a paraphrase or task test where possible. Readability scores are a weak signal only (Flesch-Kincaid grade = 0.39 x words per sentence + 11.8 x syllables per word - 15.59); never report one as proof of clarity.

## Checklist
- After the first paragraph, can the reader state the main point and their next action?
- Every heading says what follows; long sections have subheadings.
- Every sentence over about 25 words split, listed or justified; no double negatives; no undefined acronyms.
- Numbers as digits; unambiguous dates (24 October 2026); amounts with currency and units.
- Obligations, conditions and deadlines carry the same legal meaning as the original.

## Output
- The revised text, then a short change note: assumed reader, main structural changes, terms defined, and any meaning you could not preserve or questions for the owner.
- For a review: findings ranked by their effect on the reader's task, each with the original, the problem and a rewrite.

## Pitfalls
- Dropping necessary precision: plain language keeps accuracy.
- Chasing a readability score by chopping sentences into fragments.
- Silently changing a legal or safety obligation; flag such rewrites for the owner.
- Overusing bold, underline and capitals; all-caps text is harder to read.
