## When to use
Defining or sharpening how an offer competes, writing messaging for a launch or sales team, deciding how a new product is branded, or diagnosing brand strength.

## Method
1. Inputs: target segment and its job to be done, competitive alternatives (including doing nothing or a spreadsheet), your unique attributes, and proof for each.
2. Work from alternatives (April Dunford's sequence): list what customers would use instead, the attributes only you have, the value those attributes create, the customers who care most, then the market frame (category) that makes that value obvious.
3. Points of parity: what you must match to be considered in the frame. Points of difference: associations that are desirable to the target, deliverable by you, and differentiating from rivals. Two or three, never ten.
4. Positioning statement (internal, not a tagline): For <target> who <job or need>, <brand> is the <frame of reference> that <key benefit>, because <reason to believe>. Unlike <main alternative>, it <difference>.
5. Value Proposition Canvas: customer profile (jobs, pains, gains) against value map (products and services, pain relievers, gain creators). Rank pains and gains by severity; fit means the top-ranked ones are addressed. Treat fit as unproven until customer evidence backs it: interviews, landing-page and pricing-page tests.
6. Messaging house: roof = one-sentence value proposition; three pillars (benefits), each with 2-3 proof points (metrics, customer evidence, third-party validation, capabilities); foundation = brand personality, tone of voice, company credentials. Per persona, keep the roof and reorder pillars and proof.
7. Brand architecture: branded house (one master brand; efficient, shared risk), endorsed or sub-brand (parent lends credibility), house of brands (independent brands; precise targeting, highest cost). Decide by: does the master brand add credibility here, could the offer damage it, is there a segment or channel conflict, is there budget to build a new name.

## Brand equity (Keller CBBE)
- Identity: salience (breadth and depth of awareness). Meaning: performance (rational) and imagery (emotional). Response: judgments (quality, credibility, consideration, superiority) and feelings. Relationships: resonance (behavioural loyalty, attitudinal attachment, community, active engagement).
- Build bottom-up; resonance needs the lower blocks in place.
- Tracker: unaided and aided awareness, consideration, preference, purchase, repeat (report stage-to-stage conversion), attribute associations versus rivals, share of search, price premium, distinctive asset recognition, NPS (promoters 9-10 minus detractors 0-6) with its limits. Same sample design every wave.

## Output
Positioning statement; points of parity and difference with proof; canvas; messaging house table; architecture recommendation with criteria; brand health baseline and targets; test plan.

## Pitfalls
- Claiming "best" or "all-in-one for everyone": no target, no frame, no proof.
- Points of difference that buyers do not value or cannot verify.
- Messaging written from features instead of the customer job.
- Comparative or superiority claims without a reasonable basis held before publishing: FTC substantiation policy and Lanham Act s.43(a) (US), Directive 2006/114/EC (EU), CAP Code (UK). Route to legal review.
- Creating sub-brands nobody funds.
